# ISO 9241-210:2019: human-centred design

Use when planning research, prototyping, user involvement, or iterative evaluation across a product's lifecycle.

## Source and scope

[ISO 9241-210:2019](https://www.iso.org/standard/77520.html), edition 2, remains current after confirmation in 2025. It addresses human-centred design principles and activities, without supplying a complete method handbook or detailed safety assessment.

The [public contents, page iii](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/ISO/preview_ISO%2B9241-210-2019.pdf#page=3), identify six principle topics in 5.2-5.7 and activities in 7.2-7.5. The labels and checks below are **original paraphrases and practical applications**, not quoted clauses or a compliance checklist. Public sources checked 2026-11-15; the licensed full standard was not reviewed or bundled.

## Six principle topics, applied

### Ground design in users, tasks, and surroundings (5.2)

- **Apply:** investigate who uses the system, what they need to accomplish, and under what constraints before selecting a layout.
- **Example:** observe how administrators diagnose access failures instead of assuming the internal permission hierarchy is their mental model.
- **Check:** connect each important design choice to evidence or an explicitly untested assumption.

### Involve users across development (5.3)

- **Apply:** plan user participation at decisions that can still change the result, including early concepts and later working flows.
- **Example:** include both frequent and occasional operators when evaluating a new monitoring view.
- **Check:** record whose perspectives were included, what changed, and which user groups remain unrepresented.

### Let user evaluation shape revisions (5.4)

- **Apply:** evaluate against user needs and use the findings to refine the design, not merely endorse a completed build.
- **Example:** participants repeatedly misread inherited access, so revise the explanation and test the same task again.
- **Check:** trace findings to changes or a documented decision to accept a limitation.

### Iterate when evidence reveals gaps (5.5)

- **Apply:** revisit requirements and solutions as understanding improves. Keep prototypes cheap enough to change.
- **Example:** compare two permission explanations before implementing the more successful one.
- **Check:** retest the risky task after revision; a completed review meeting is not evidence the issue is fixed.

### Consider the complete experience (5.6)

- **Apply:** include setup, ordinary work, interruptions, errors, recovery, and later use rather than optimizing one ideal screen.
- **Example:** evaluate an indexing workflow from source setup through a failed run and recovery, including help and handoff.
- **Check:** identify missing journey stages and record how failures affect the user's wider goal.

### Bring different disciplines and perspectives (5.7)

- **Apply:** include relevant domain, research, design, engineering, accessibility, and operational expertise. Choose participants for the problem, not a fixed department list.
- **Example:** a security specialist checks authorization behavior while users and accessibility reviewers evaluate how denied access is explained.
- **Check:** assign responsibility for unresolved questions and verify that specialist findings affect the design.

## Turn the activities into a repeatable loop

For sections 7.2-7.5, our working loop is: describe the context, define user requirements, make solutions concrete, then evaluate them. Revisit any earlier step when findings invalidate an assumption; do not treat this as a one-pass waterfall.

**Completion evidence:** retain the context, requirements, prototype/version, evaluation observations, decisions, and next test. Use [ISO 9241-11](iso-9241-11.md) for outcome measures and [WCAG](wcag-22.md) for accessibility checks. An internal opinion or automated scan does not replace user evaluation of the actual task.
